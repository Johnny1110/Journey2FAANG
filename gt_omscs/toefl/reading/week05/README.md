### Passage 1 — Paths Nobody Planned

Almost every park and campus has them: narrow strips of bare earth worn across the grass by people who decided that the paved route was too long. Planners call them desire paths. They record, more honestly than any survey, where people actually want to go.

For a long time the official response was to block them. Fences, hedges and other obstacles went up, signs asked walkers to keep off the grass, and fresh turf was laid over the worn strips. The paths usually came back within weeks. A shortcut that saves even thirty seconds may be used by hundreds of people a day, and the grass never has time to recover between their steps.

Some designers have since taken the opposite approach. Instead of guessing where a path should run, they leave a new lawn unpaved for a season, watch where the lines appear, and then pave those. The method costs little, and it produces routes that people actually follow.

Not every desire path deserves to be paved, of course. Some cut across fragile ground; others lead walkers straight into traffic. But even these remain useful, because they show a planner exactly where the original design failed the people it was meant to serve.

### Passage 2 — The Pendulum That Proved the Earth Turns

By 1851 no serious scientist doubted that the Earth rotated. What nobody had managed was to show it directly, so that an ordinary person could see the motion with their own eyes. That year the French physicist Léon Foucault did exactly that, with an apparatus so simple it seemed almost absurd: a heavy metal ball hung from a long wire.

A pendulum, once set swinging, keeps moving back and forth in the same plane unless some force pushes it aside. Foucault reasoned that if the Earth turned beneath the pendulum, the floor would turn while the swing stayed fixed. To people standing on that floor, the pendulum would seem to change direction slowly over the day.

After early trials in his own cellar, he hung a 28-kilogram ball from a 67-metre wire under the dome of the Panthéon in Paris. A pin beneath the ball traced each swing in a ring of sand on the floor, and visitors watched the line creep around the circle at roughly eleven degrees an hour.

The rate depends on latitude. At the North Pole the line would go all the way round in about a day; at the equator it would not turn at all. Foucault's apparatus, in other words, showed not only that the Earth turns but roughly where on its surface the observer is standing.
