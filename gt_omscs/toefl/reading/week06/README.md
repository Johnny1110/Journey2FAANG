### Passage 1 — Learning to Paint Distance

Paintings from medieval Europe often look strangely flat to modern eyes. Figures were usually sized by importance rather than by distance, so a saint might tower over the building behind him. Painters knew that faraway objects look smaller, but they had no reliable method for showing it.

That changed in Florence in the early 1400s. The architect Filippo Brunelleschi is credited with the first demonstration of linear perspective, a system in which parallel lines, such as the edges of a road, meet at a single vanishing point. He painted a church on a small wooden panel and drilled a hole through the middle. A viewer stood behind the panel, looked through the hole and saw the painting reflected in a mirror. When the mirror was lowered, the real church appeared exactly where the painted one had been.

The panel itself has been lost, and the experiment is known only from an account written decades later by one of Brunelleschi's admirers. Some historians therefore question how much of it happened as described.

What is not disputed is how quickly the idea spread. In 1435 the scholar Leon Battista Alberti set out the rules in a short book for painters, and within a few decades artists across Italy were using vanishing points to create a convincing sense of depth. Perspective did more than make pictures look real. It placed the viewer at one fixed point, as if every painting were a window that a single person was looking through.

### Passage 2 — Why Cities Stay Warm at Night

On a calm summer evening, the center of a large city can be several degrees warmer than the countryside around it. Scientists call this the urban heat island effect, and it has several causes.

Dark surfaces such as asphalt and roofs absorb sunlight during the day and release the stored heat slowly after sunset. Tall buildings trap this heat and block winds that might otherwise carry it away. Cities also have fewer trees and less open soil. In the countryside, plants draw water from the ground and release it through their leaves; as the water evaporates, it cools the surrounding air, much as sweat cools the skin. Finally, cars, air conditioners and factories add waste heat of their own.

The effect is usually strongest at night rather than at noon. After sunset the countryside loses its heat quickly, while the city keeps releasing what it stored during the day. This matters for health. During a heat wave, most people can cope with a hot afternoon if they are able to recover overnight; in the center of a city, that relief may never come.

Cities have begun to respond. Some paint roofs white or light gray so that they reflect sunlight instead of absorbing it; others plant trees along their streets. Neither measure removes the effect altogether, but studies suggest that both can lower local temperatures by a few degrees.
