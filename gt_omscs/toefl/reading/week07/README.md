### Passage 1 — The Musician Who Plays Nothing

For most of the history of Western music, orchestras had no conductor in the modern sense. A small ensemble was usually led by one of its own players, often the first violinist or the musician at the keyboard, who kept everyone together with nods and small gestures while playing.

Some leaders beat time more forcefully. In 1687 the French composer Jean-Baptiste Lully was marking the beat with a heavy staff, pounding it on the floor, when he struck his own foot. The wound became infected, and he died a few months later. Ironically, the tool he used to keep musicians in order cost him his life.

In the nineteenth century, orchestras grew much larger, and new works by composers such as Beethoven and Wagner changed speed and volume constantly. A player buried in the violin section could no longer hear, let alone see, everyone else. A leader who stood in front, played nothing and held only a light stick, the baton, became necessary. Who introduced the baton first remains disputed; several musicians later claimed the honor.

Once in place, the conductor did far more than keep time. By deciding how fast a passage should move and which instrument should stand out, conductors became interpreters, and audiences began to buy tickets to hear a particular conductor's version of a familiar symphony.

### Passage 2 — Why Airplane Windows Have Round Corners

In 1952 the de Havilland Comet became the first jet airliner to carry paying passengers. It flew higher and faster than any propeller plane, and for a short time it was the pride of British engineering. Then, in 1954, two Comets broke apart in mid-air within four months, and the whole fleet was grounded.

To find the cause, investigators placed an entire Comet body in a huge tank of water and pumped water in and out of the cabin again and again. Each cycle imitated one flight. In the air, a cabin is pressurized so that passengers can breathe, and the metal stretches slightly; on the ground, it relaxes again. After the equivalent of about three thousand flights, the metal split open.

The failure began at the corner of an opening cut into the body. Sharp corners concentrate stress: a force that is spread evenly across a smooth surface piles up wherever an edge turns suddenly. With every flight, tiny cracks grew at these points, too faint to notice, until the metal finally tore. Engineers call this process metal fatigue.

The lesson changed aircraft design. Openings in later airliners were given rounded corners, which spread the stress more evenly, and new designs were tested for fatigue over many thousands of pressure cycles. That is why the windows of every passenger jet today have rounded corners.
