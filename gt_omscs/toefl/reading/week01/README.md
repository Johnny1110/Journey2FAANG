### Passage 1 — Reading the Layers

Beneath every riverbed and lake lies a record of the past. Each year, tiny particles of rock, sand, and organic material settle to the bottom, forming a thin band called a sediment layer. Over centuries these bands stack one upon another, and geologists can read them much as one reads the pages of a book.

The principle behind the method is simple: in undisturbed ground, older layers lie below younger ones. By counting downward, scientists assign an approximate date to each band. The layers also preserve clues about the climate of their time. A band rich in pollen suggests a warm, forested period, while one full of coarse gravel points to heavy flooding.

This record is not always easy to interpret. Earthquakes can fold or overturn entire sections, placing old material above new. Burrowing animals mix particles from different periods. For this reason, researchers rarely rely on a single site. They compare cores drilled from several locations, and only when the same pattern appears in all of them do they treat the sequence as reliable. Such caution has made sediment analysis one of the most trusted tools for reconstructing the Earth's distant past.

### Passage 2 — The London Coffeehouse

When the first coffeehouse opened in London in 1652, few expected it to change the way the city did business. Yet within thirty years more than two thousand had appeared, and they had become the informal offices of the entire capital.

Their appeal lay partly in price. For one penny, anyone could enter, read the newspapers provided, and join the conversation at any table. Unlike taverns, where rank determined seating, coffeehouses seated customers wherever there was room. A merchant might find himself debating politics with a poet or a ship's captain.

This mixing had practical consequences. Traders who gathered at one house near the docks began posting news of arriving ships; that establishment eventually became Lloyd's, the great insurance market. Another, frequented by scientists, hosted discussions that fed directly into the early Royal Society.

Not everyone approved. Charles II tried to close the coffeehouses in 1675, arguing that they spread dangerous rumours, but the order was withdrawn within days after fierce public protest. They had already become too useful to abandon. By the eighteenth century their function had begun to divide: some evolved into private clubs, others into stock exchanges and auction houses that still operate today.
