### Passage 1 — The Layout That Outlived Its Machine

The keyboard in front of you was designed for a machine that no longer exists. In the 1870s a typewriter's metal typebars swung up to strike the paper from below, and two bars struck in quick succession would jam against each other. Christopher Sholes broke up the letter pairs most common in English and scattered them across the board. The layout that resulted, QWERTY, made typing a little slower and jamming far rarer.

The typebars are long gone. The layout has held up anyway. Rival arrangements have been proposed for a century — the best known, Dvorak's, puts the most frequent letters under the strongest fingers — and none has displaced it.

Economists call this path dependence: an early choice, made for reasons that have since disappeared, locks a whole system in place. The obstacle is not that QWERTY is good; it is that everyone already knows it. A firm that changed its keyboards would have to retrain its typists, and a typist who learned Dvorak would be slow on every other machine in the country.

Whether Dvorak is measurably faster remains disputed, and the careful trials show a small advantage at best. But the argument does not depend on the size of that advantage. Even a clearly better layout would face the same problem: the cost of switching falls on whoever moves first, while the benefit arrives only once everyone else has followed.

### Passage 2 — Selling the Cold

In 1806 a young Boston merchant named Frederic Tudor loaded a ship with blocks cut from a New England pond and sailed for the Caribbean. Almost everyone he knew thought the plan absurd: the cargo would melt long before it arrived, and nobody in Martinique had asked for ice. Most of that first shipment did melt, and Tudor spent the next several years in and out of debtors' prison.

What eventually saved the business was insulation, not refrigeration. Sawdust, a waste product of the region's sawmills, turned out to hold a block of ice almost indefinitely. Packed in sawdust and kept in thick-walled ice houses, ice could reach Calcutta — a voyage of four months — with two thirds of its weight intact.

Tudor's harder problem was demand. Ice was not something anyone in a hot city had grown up wanting. He gave it away to bartenders and to hospitals, on the theory that a customer who had once been served a cold drink would not go back. The theory held. By the 1860s ice was an ordinary household expense in New Orleans, and the ponds of Massachusetts were being harvested every winter by thousands of men.

The industry then vanished within a single generation. Mechanical refrigeration did not depend on a cold winter or a distant pond, and by 1900 it had made the whole apparatus of saws, sawdust and sailing ships unnecessary.
