### Passage 1 — The Cables Beneath the Sea

Most people picture the internet as something wireless, carried by satellites overhead. In fact, more than ninety-five percent of international data travels through cables lying on the ocean floor. A modern cable is no thicker than a garden hose, yet a single one can carry the traffic of an entire continent.

The idea is older than it sounds. The first working telegraph cable across the Atlantic was completed in 1866. It could carry neither speech nor images, only a few words per minute in Morse code. Today's cables use hair-thin glass fibres and pulses of light, but the route they follow is much the same, because the seabed along it is relatively flat.

Laying a cable is slow and expensive; repairing one is worse. A ship must locate the break, lift the cable from depths of several kilometres, and splice it at the surface. Most damage, however, is not caused by earthquakes or sharks, as is often reported, but by fishing gear and ship anchors in shallow water near the coast. For this reason, operators run several separate cables to each landing point. When one fails, traffic shifts to another within seconds, and users on shore notice nothing at all.

### Passage 2 — Cleaning the Past

When conservators removed centuries of grime from Michelangelo's ceiling in the Sistine Chapel during the 1980s, the colours that emerged startled the art world. The figures, long admired for their solemn, shadowed tones, turned out to be dressed in bright pink, green, and orange.

Not everyone welcomed the result. Some critics argued that the darker surface was not merely dirt but a deliberate final layer, applied by the artist himself to soften the contrasts. Remove it, they said, and you remove part of the painting. The conservators replied that chemical analysis had found no such layer — only candle soot, glue from earlier repairs, and the residue of previous cleanings.

The dispute exposed a problem that has no perfect solution. Every painting begins to change the moment it is finished: varnish yellows, pigments fade, and canvas darkens. A restorer must therefore decide which moment in a work's life counts as the true one. Returning a painting to its first day ignores four centuries of history; leaving it untouched allows the artist's intentions to disappear under a brown film. Most museums now favour the least invasive treatment possible, and, just as importantly, they document every step in detail.
